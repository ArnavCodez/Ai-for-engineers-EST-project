What the models say

M3 is our final model: MobileNetV2 pretrained on 1.2 million ImageNet photos, then fine-tuned on steel. M1 is the small CNN we built and trained from scratch.

Inside the network, one step at a time

Everything below is computed live for the image above. Change the image or move a slider and every number updates. We follow M1 because it is small enough to show every layer. M3 works the same way with more layers.

Move the mouse over the image. The box marks the pixels shown on the right.

A computer sees an image as a grid of numbers. Our images are 200 × 200 pixels in grayscale, so each one is 40,000 numbers, one per pixel: 0 is black and 255 is white.

M1 divides every number by 255 so they run from 0 to 1. M3 was pretrained on colour photos of 224 × 224, so the image is enlarged to 224 × 224, copied into three identical colour channels and scaled to −1 to 1.

Move over the image to place the 3 × 3 filter.

The 32 filters of M1's first layer positive weight negative weight

A filter is a small grid of 9 learned numbers (its weights) plus one bias. It slides over every position of the image. At each position it multiplies the 3 × 3 pixels under it by its 9 weights, adds the products and the bias, and keeps the result only if it is positive. That last step is called ReLU: max(0, value).

Doing this at all 40,000 positions gives a new 200 × 200 image called a feature map. Dark blue places in the map are where the pattern this filter responds to appears. Nobody wrote these weights by hand: training adjusted them over many passes through the 1,260 training images until the predictions were right.

This is Grad-CAM. It takes the feature maps of the model's last convolution layer and asks how much the score of the predicted class would rise if each map got stronger. That rate of change is the gradient.

Each map is weighted by its average gradient, the weighted maps are added, and only the positive part is kept. The result is enlarged to the image size. Yellow means that part of the image pushed the model towards its answer.

On most images M3 looks at the defect itself, while M1 often looks at the background texture. M1 also breaks down much sooner on darker or noisy images, as the robustness chart below shows.

About the data and the project

The dataset

The NEU surface defect database from Northeastern University, China: 1,800 grayscale images of hot-rolled steel strip, 200 × 200 pixels, 300 per class. We used the Kaggle mirror and only the image class, not the detection boxes.

We split it once, before looking at the images: 1,260 for training, 270 for validation and 270 for testing, the same share of every class in each part (seed 42). The one duplicate pair we found was kept inside one part.

The three models

M1 is our own CNN: three convolution blocks, 109,958 learned numbers. M2 adds batch normalisation and data augmentation. M3 is MobileNetV2 pretrained on ImageNet with a new six-class output, fine-tuned on steel, 2,265,670 learned numbers.

The models were trained on Kaggle. Everything on this page runs on your own computer, with no server, using TensorFlow.js.
